Barbershop style

What makes a chord ring?

Barbershop is a uniquely American style of a cappella singing: four voices, no instruments, close harmony on nearly every melody note. When those four voices tune precisely, their overtones reinforce one another and the chord seems to “ring” — many listeners hear a note that no one is actually singing.

The four parts

Women's quartets use the same part names as men's — tenor, lead, baritone and bass — with every part voiced in women's ranges. The arrangement, not the singer, sets the rules.

Lead

Usually carries the melody and sets the emotional delivery of the song. The other three parts tune to the lead.

Tenor

Harmonizes above the melody, typically the highest voice, sung with a lighter, brighter tone so it sparkles over the chord.

Baritone

Fills in whichever note completes the chord, crossing above and below the lead — often the trickiest part to learn.

Bass

The lowest voice, singing mostly roots and fifths. A solid, well-tuned bass gives the other three something to lock onto.

How to recognize it

Listen for these hallmarks in any of our videos.

The barbershop seventh
The signature chord — a dominant seventh — appears far more often here than in other styles.
Lock and ring
Singers tune by ear rather than to a piano, so chords lock in place and produce audible overtones.
Swipes
The chord changes underneath a single held word or syllable.
Tags
A dramatic ending, often with one part holding a long note while the others move around it.
One voice, four parts
All four parts usually sing the same words at the same time, so the lyrics stay clear.
Woodshedding
Making up harmony by ear, without sheet music — the way barbershop began.

A short history

Barbershop harmony grew out of informal close-harmony singing in late-1800s America, with deep roots in African American vocal traditions. Neighborhood barbershops were gathering places where men harmonized while they waited — and the name stuck.

The style was revived and preserved by singing societies in the twentieth century. Women formed organizations of their own, including Harmony, Inc., founded in 1959 and still home to women's quartets and choruses across North America — High Gear among them.
